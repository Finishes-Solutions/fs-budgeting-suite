How to Use the Budgeting Suite

Build a multifamily project budget from the shared pricing library, then export it.

What This Is

A Business Development budgeting tool for multifamily projects. You enter the project's size, unit mix and income assumptions; the tool prices the construction scope from a shared, versioned rate library and rolls it up into two numbers that matter:

  • Contract sum — construction hard cost + profit + contingency. This is the number the bank sees.
  • Total project cost — contract sum + soft costs + land + developer fee, marketing and closing. Internal underwriting only.

Every saved budget also writes a normalized schedule-of-values table that the Master Lender Package pipeline reads later, so the line items you set here become the SOV rows in the lender package.

Quick Start

1
Home → pick a starting point. + New Multifamily Budget copies the current pricing library and the standard template — unit mix, income, expenses and every priced line. + Blank Budget starts genuinely empty: no units, no income, no scope, no square footage. Use blank when the project is not multifamily or looks nothing like the template, and build the scope yourself from the CSI catalog.
2
Fill in the Inputs page. Project ID and name first — the ID is required before anything can be saved. Then residential and amenity square footage, the unit mix, income and expenses, and the land and project costs that sit outside the construction contract.
3
Continue to budget. Every line is already priced from the library. Adjust quantities or rates where you know better, and add scope with + Add line item.
4
Check the summary. Contract sum, total project cost, cost per unit and the SOV rollup are at the bottom of the page.
5
Save, then export. Save writes to the database; Export… produces Excel or PDF.
The header pill shows Unsaved changes until a save succeeds. Nothing is stored automatically — if you close the tab with that pill showing, the browser will warn you. Cmd+S (or Ctrl+S) saves from anywhere in a budget.

Project IDs

The ID is the primary key for the budget, in the database and in every downstream system. It cannot be changed by renaming later without creating a second record, so get it right at the start.

OP-####Operations project — four digits, e.g. OP-0001.
BD-####Business Development pursuit — four digits, e.g. BD-0001.
AS-####Assessment — four digits, e.g. AS-0001.
TST-####Test budget. Use TST-9999 when checking a preview build.
Test budgets are real rows. Preview builds share the production database, so a TST- budget is written to the same tables as live projects. It is flagged with a Test badge everywhere it appears — delete it when you are finished.

Inputs Reference

Project

Residential buildable SF drives every line priced per residential SF — the bulk of the budget and most soft costs. Amenity / clubhouse SF drives the amenity scope at its own, higher rates, because the model prices the two buildings separately.

Unit Mix & Rent Revenue

One row per unit type. Type SF, monthly and yearly revenue are calculated. Two behaviors worth knowing:

  • Editing $ / SF / mo works backwards and sets rent to SF × your rate, matching the direction the source spreadsheet calculates.
  • The badge in the card header shows an efficiency percentage — explained below.

What Efficiency Means

Efficiency is the share of the building you can actually rent:

efficiency = total unit SF ÷ residential buildable SF

Total unit SF is every unit type's SF multiplied by its count — the leasable area. Residential buildable SF is the whole structure. The gap between them is everything you build but nobody rents: corridors, stairwells, elevator shafts, lobbies, walls, mechanical and electrical rooms, trash rooms.

It matters because you pay to build the whole number and collect rent on only part of it. Two buildings of identical size at different efficiencies produce different revenue from the same construction cost, so it is one of the earliest signals of whether a layout works.

Typical rangeGarden-style walk-ups tend to run high, often 85% or better, because they have little interior circulation. Mid-rise with double-loaded corridors and elevators usually sits lower. What counts as good is specific to the building type — compare against your own past projects rather than a rule of thumb.
Reading it lowEither the design carries a lot of unrentable area, or the unit mix is incomplete — a missing unit type or a wrong count will understate leasable SF and make an ordinary building look inefficient.
Amber warningUnit SF exceeds residential buildable SF, which cannot be true. Almost always a typo in a unit count or SF, or a residential SF figure that was never updated after the design changed.

Amenity SF is deliberately excluded from this calculation. The clubhouse is not rentable area, and it is measured and priced separately.

Site Efficiency, and How It Differs

Building efficiency asks how much of the structure you can rent. Site efficiency asks how hard the land is working:

site efficiency = (residential SF + amenity SF) ÷ total site SF

That is the floor area ratio, expressed as a percentage. Enter the land area in Total Site SF and the Project card reports site area in acres, site efficiency, density in units per acre, and building efficiency side by side.

What it tells youWhether the scheme is using the parcel you are paying for. A low figure on expensive land is the earliest sign that the deal needs more density, or that the land is priced wrong for what will fit on it.
DensityUnits per acre, the number entitlement and zoning conversations are usually conducted in. It comes from the same site figure, so it appears as soon as you enter one.
Over 100%The tile turns amber. Building SF exceeding site SF is normal for anything multi-storey — a two-storey building covering half the site is already 100%. Read it as a ratio, not a ceiling; it is only a warning when you expected a single-storey scheme.

Total site SF prices nothing. It never touches a rate, a quantity or the contract sum — it exists purely to produce these measures, so leaving it at 0 costs you nothing but the metrics.

Other Income and Operating Expenses

Enter gross charges under Other income — what the resident is billed. Put the cost of providing them under Operating expenses. The tool nets the two and reports net other income.

  • A green ● next to a count means it tracks total units automatically. Type over the count to break the link — useful for parking spaces, pet rent or premium units that apply to only some units.
  • Expenses are per unit or fixed / mo. Fixed lines apply once no matter the unit count.

Profit & Contingency

Profit is a percentage of construction hard cost, per company; add a row for each entity taking a fee. Contingency carries three ways — percent of hard cost, dollars per SF (residential + amenity), or a fixed amount. It is always reported as its own line, never blended into the trades.

The Budget Page

Lines are grouped into three scopes — Residential Construction, Amenity / Clubhouse and Soft Costs — and then into phase groups within each. Click any scope or group header to collapse it; subtotals stay visible. Collapse all is the fastest way to sanity-check the shape of a budget.

Quantities

A green ● on a quantity means it is derived from the inputs, so it follows your SF and unit counts automatically:

per res SFQuantity = residential buildable SF
per amen SFQuantity = amenity SF
per unitQuantity = total units from the unit mix
lump sumQuantity is always 1; put the whole amount in the rate

Typing over a quantity breaks the link for that line only, and it will no longer follow the inputs.

Manually Edited Lines

A blue dot to the left of a line means you changed it rather than taking the library's number. A line is marked when you override its quantity, set a rate that differs from the pricing library, change its description or basis, or add it by hand. Hover the dot to see which of those applied. The count of edited lines sits in the card header.

Put a rate back to the library figure and the dot clears itself, so the marks stay honest. They also travel into the Excel export, which carries a Manually edited column alongside the original library rate — useful when someone asks which numbers came from the model and which came from judgment.

Scopes, and How They Relate to CSI Codes

Every line belongs to one of three scopes. The scope answers where the money goes; the CSI code answers what the work is. They are independent, which is why the same code legitimately appears more than once.

Residential ConstructionThe apartment buildings themselves. Priced against residential buildable SF. Inside the construction contract.
Amenity / ClubhouseThe clubhouse and leasing building. Priced against amenity SF at its own, higher rates. Also inside the construction contract.
Soft CostsDesign, engineering, permits, impact fees, contingencies on those. Priced against residential SF, but outside the construction contract — it is part of total project cost only.

So Residential + Amenity = construction hard cost, which becomes the contract sum once profit and contingency are added. Soft costs never enter that number.

No clubhouse? Set amenity SF to 0. The amenity scope then disappears from the budget, the CSI schedule and both exports, and stops contributing to the contract sum — including any amenity line you had set to a lump sum or given a manual quantity, which would otherwise keep adding money you could no longer see. Nothing is deleted: put a figure back in amenity SF and the whole scope returns exactly as it was.

Why the Same CSI Code Appears Twice

Take 09 60 00 Flooring. It exists in the residential scope at one rate and in the amenity scope at a much higher one, because clubhouse flooring is a different specification from apartment flooring. One CSI code, two scopes, two rates — that is correct, not a duplicate. On the CSI schedule both lines sit under Division 09 with a Residential or Amenity tag telling you which building each belongs to.

Soft costs use SC-## codes rather than CSI codes, because MasterFormat classifies construction work results — it has no home for an architect's fee or a traffic impact study. That is the second reason they are reported separately on the schedule.

Scope and Basis When Adding a Line

The scope dropdown in the CSI picker sets the quantity basis for you: choosing Amenity gives the line per amen SF, anything else gives per res SF. Change it afterwards with the Basis dropdown if the line should count per unit or be a lump sum instead. Getting the scope right matters beyond bookkeeping — it decides which square footage drives the quantity, and whether the money lands inside or outside the number the bank sees.

Adding Scope

+ Add Line Item opens the CSI MasterFormat catalog. Adding from the catalog means the line carries a real code into the SOV, which the lender package depends on. New lines arrive at rate $0 — set the rate before you trust the total.

The catalog is the same MasterFormat list wherever you open it — the fields above the search box decide where the new line goes, not what you can pick from.

Add To is the scope: whose cost the line is. The CSI code says what the work is; the scope says which bucket the money lands in — and the same code can legitimately appear in more than one scope. The standard template prices 28 46 00 Fire Detection & Alarm twice: once for the residential building and once for the clubhouse, at different rates.

Residential ConstructionPriced against residential SF by default. Rolls into hard cost and the contract sum.
Amenity / ClubhousePriced against amenity SF. Rolls into hard cost — but when amenity SF is 0 there is no amenity building, and the whole scope contributes nothing.
Soft CostsOutside the construction contract sum. Joins at total project cost, and is reported separately on the SOV.

Group picks the heading the line files under:

An existing groupThe line is filed under that heading — pick Site Finish and it lands with the other site finish lines instead of starting a section of its own. The list shows only groups already in use for the chosen scope, so it follows the Add To dropdown.
AutoThe default. The line is filed under its CSI division, creating a heading such as "Division 14 — Conveying Equipment".
New group…Reveals a New Group Name box so you can type your own heading, for scope that does not belong anywhere existing.

Lines are inserted next to the group they join rather than appended at the end, so a group never appears twice in the budget or in the exports.

Reading the Summary

The left column walks hard cost up to contract sum, then adds the non-contract costs to reach total project cost. The right column is the SOV rollup: the same money grouped the way it will appear on the schedule of values, with contingency shown explicitly as line 99.03 and profit broken out by company.

CSI Schedule

The CSI schedule tab re-presents the same budget as a MasterFormat schedule of values: sorted by division number, grouped under Division 00–48 headings with a subtotal per division, showing the full code on every line. Same money as the Budget page, organised the way an architect, lender or general contractor expects to receive it.

  • Profit and contingency appear as Division 99 — Markups, with contingency carried explicitly as 99 03 00 and never blended into a trade line.
  • Soft costs are listed separately and are not part of the contract sum — the bank sees the construction contract, not the total project cost.
  • Hand-added lines with no CSI code collect under Uncoded / Custom Scope, a prompt to give them a real code before the budget goes out.

The Residential and Amenity tags on each line say which building the work belongs to, so a code that appears in both scopes stays readable. See The Budget Page for what the scopes mean and why a code can appear twice.

The schedule is also its own Excel sheet and its own PDF section, both selectable at export.

Pricing Library and Versioning

All rates come from one shared library so two people pricing the same building get the same answer. The important rule:

A budget snapshots the library the moment it is created. Publishing new rates never moves the numbers under an existing budget. Your saved work does not change behind your back.

When a budget is running on an older library, a banner appears on the budget page naming both versions. Update to current pricing pulls the new rates in — it matches on scope, code and description, so standard template lines are repriced and any custom line you added keeps the rate you gave it. Quantities and overrides are untouched.

Publishing is the other direction: an admin edits the library and publishes, which stamps a new version number and effective date. Only budgets created after that point pick it up.

Investment Analysis

The Investment Analysis tab turns a construction budget into a five- or ten-year before-tax underwriting model: operating cash flows, financing, hypothetical sales, and the returns an investor would look at — cash-on-cash, DSCR, equity multiple, IRRs, leverage, and an acquisition-price sensitivity table.

Starting one. Open a budget and press Create Investment Analysis. The budget is saved first, and the analysis imports from that exact saved version — the property name and address, the size, the unit mix, gross scheduled rent (unit rents × 12, never the budget's net income figure), other-income rows, and the total project cost as a suggested purchase price. Every imported value shows a green From Budget badge and stays fully editable. A standalone analysis, with nothing imported, starts from the Investment Analysis tab.

The import is a snapshot, not a live link. Each analysis records the exact budget version it came from. Changing the budget later never changes the analysis.

The six steps. Work left to right: Source & Property, Income, Vacancy & Expenses, Mortgage, Disposition, then Review & Report. Each step ends with a Next button that lights up green when the step's required fields are filled, and completed steps earn a checkmark on their pill. Three things are never guessed for you and must be entered: the purchase month, the property's complete operating expenses (the budget's service-expense rows appear as a partial reference only), and the exit cap rate. Financing is either all cash or one fully amortizing first mortgage entered as dollars or LTV. The projection runs five or ten years — pick on the first step.

Calculate and Save. Calculate sends the assumptions to the server and shows the report; the browser never computes results itself. Editing any input marks the result stale until you recalculate. Save (or Cmd/Ctrl+S) recalculates and stores the analysis — every meaningful save records an immutable version, and a save that changed nothing records none, exactly like budgets. If someone else saved the same analysis after you opened it, your save is refused with the option to reload their version.

Versions. Open version history from the three-dot menu on an analysis row — on the Home page list or the Investment Analysis tab — the same way budget versions work. Old versions open read-only, exactly as calculated when saved, and are never recalculated by newer formulas.

Reading the report. A few conventions worth knowing: a sale at the end of a year is priced on the following year's income at the exit cap rate, rounded to the nearest $1,000, and selling costs and the loan payoff come out of that rounded price. Leverage compares the equity IRR against the unleveraged property IRR — positive leverage means borrowing raised the return. The optimal holding period is simply the sale year with the highest equity IRR.

Exports. Export… works like the budget's: pick which sections to include, then produce either the branded PDF report or an Excel workbook of real numeric cells. Ticking Highlight Key Metrics puts a light green highlight on the PDF's gross operating income, net operating income, purchase price, initial investment, equity multiple, cash-on-cash and IRR so they stand out at a glance. Both formats are built from the calculated result and the button is disabled while the result is stale, so an export can never disagree with the assumptions on screen. On phones and small screens analyses are view-only, like the rest of the suite.

Who Can Do What

EveryoneAnyone who can sign in with a Finishes account can create, edit, save, delete and export budgets, and can read the pricing library.
pricing_adminAdditionally can edit and publish the pricing library. This is deliberately a very short list, because a rate change affects every budget created afterwards.

The pricing page shows whether your account holds the role. If you need it, ask the tool owner for a role invitation.

Troubleshooting

A red banner says the server cannot be reached Saves will fail and rates shown may be the built-in fallback rather than the published library. Reload. If it persists, the API or database is down — report it before doing more work, and copy anything unsaved out first.
The first request of the day hangs The database pauses when idle and takes a few seconds to wake. Retry once.
Publishing pricing returns "not authorized" Your account does not hold pricing_admin in this browser profile. Check /.auth/me; if the role is missing, the role invitation needs to be accepted in the profile you are actually signed in with.
Sign-in loops back to the login page Usually the address you are using is not registered as a redirect URI — common on a preview build. Send the URL to whoever set up the environment.
A budget shows $0 totals Almost always residential SF is 0, or every line was switched to lump sum with no rate set.
I lost my edits Nothing autosaves. The header pill shows unsaved state and the browser warns before closing — if that warning was dismissed, the work is gone.
